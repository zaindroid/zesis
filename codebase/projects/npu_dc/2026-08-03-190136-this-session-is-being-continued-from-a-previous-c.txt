● That pattern (short = fine, ammeter in series = nothing) points to a
  burden-voltage / inrush problem, not a wiring or firmware issue. The ammeter
  has some real series resistance; if J24 carries more current than the µA-scale
  trickle J25 saw — especially at boot, when flash init and clock startup can
  draw brief current spikes — that resistance causes enough of a voltage sag to
  brown the board out before it ever gets UART running. A direct short has ~0Ω,
  so it never sees that drop.

  Two things to check, cheapest first:

  1. Is the PPK2 app set to Auto range for Ampere Meter mode? If it's pinned to
  a low manual range (leftover from the µA-scale J25 test), it'll current-limit
  hard at just a few mA and this exact symptom follows. Switch it to Auto if it
  isn't already.
  2. Connection quality — if you're using alligator clips, they add their own
  contact resistance on top of PPK2's own. Shortest possible leads, solid
  contact directly on the J24 pads, makes a real difference here.

  If both of those check out and it still won't boot with the ammeter in line,
  the fix is a small bypass capacitor (~47–100µF) wired directly across the two
  pins on the DUT (board) side of the ammeter connection, as physically close to
  J24 as you can get it. That lets the board pull its transient/inrush current
  locally from the cap instead of through the ammeter's resistance — PPK2 then
  only sees the smoothed average current, which is exactly what we want for
  energy comparisons anyway (we care about average/total energy per operation,
  not sub-millisecond inrush spikes).

  Can you check the range setting first — what does the PPK2 app currently show
  it set to?

● That "Power Output" toggle is the tell — that belongs to PPK2's Source Meter
  mode, where PPK2 supplies its own regulated voltage to the DUT and caps the

  PPK2 becomes the board's power source for that net instead of the board's own
  LDO — so when you enable it, the board tries to run on whatever current PPK2's
  current-limit allows (sounds like it's capped around 8mA, nowhere near enough
  for a booting Cortex-M33 + flash + peripherals), and when you disable it,
  there's no supply at all, hence the near-zero 0.16µA (just leakage, nothing
  actually powered).

  Switch the app to "Ampere Meter" mode instead. In that mode PPK2 doesn't
  generate any voltage of its own — it's a pure pass-through ammeter, and
  there's no "Power Output" toggle at all, because the board's own USB-derived
  supply (still normally connected) is what's expected to flow through it.

  Wiring for Ampere Meter mode, same convention as before:
  - PPK2 VIN ← the LDO-side pad of the now-open J24 (the side still connected
  back toward the board's own regulator/USB power)
  - PPK2 VOUT → the MCU-side pad of J24 (the side that continues on to the rest
  of the board)
  - PPK2 GND ↔ board GND (common reference)

  This way the board keeps running on its normal USB-derived power exactly as
  before you cut J24 — PPK2 just measures whatever current flows through it in
  the process, no competing supply, no current cap.

● Fair question, and honestly a good one to stop and reconsider. The trigger
  pin's only purpose is to mark exact boundaries between keypair/encaps/decaps
  within the current trace, so energy can be attributed per-operation rather
  than just "handshake active vs idle." Without it, the clean current curve you
  already have only tells you total energy for the whole active window (all
  three ops back-to-back), not the breakdown between them.

  But there's a reasonable way to get that breakdown without the trigger, using
  data we already trust: we have very precise, low-variance DWT cycle counts for
  each operation (std dev <0.5% of mean, cross-validated against an independent
  run). Since keypair/encaps/decaps run sequentially in a known, fixed order,
  we can take the total energy of the active window (current × voltage × time,
  integrated) and split it proportionally by each operation's share of total
  cycles — e.g., for ML-KEM-512, keypair/encaps/decaps are roughly
  19.86/23.36/28.95 ms out of a ~72ms handshake, so allocate energy in that
  ratio.

  The honest tradeoff: this assumes power draw is roughly uniform per cycle
  across all three operations (a reasonable assumption since they share the same
  underlying NTT/polynomial arithmetic, but not something we've directly
  verified — a true trigger-based split would confirm it rather than assume it).

  Given how much friction the trigger pin has caused, I'd lean toward: use the 
  proportional-split method for now, get the actual energy numbers you need, and
  treat exact per-op triggering as a stretch goal if time permits later rather
  than a blocker. Want to go that route?

● That data is clean — completely deterministic (identical cycle count on all
  100 reps, since the Montgomery ladder used here is a fixed-length, branchless
  computation with no data-dependent timing, a genuinely nice
  side-channel-resistant property). keypair and shared_secret cost virtually the
  same, which makes sense: crypto_scalarmult_base is literally
  crypto_scalarmult called with a fixed base point — same 255-iteration ladder
  either way.

  But there's something important to flag before we present this as a
  comparison: 107,508,133 cycles = 716.7 ms per operation — about 36× slower
  than ML-KEM-512's keypair generation (19.9 ms). That's a real result, but it
  doesn't match the broader literature — X25519 is normally faster than ML-KEM
  on similar embedded hardware, not dramatically slower.

  The reason: TweetNaCl is deliberately optimized for minimal code size, not 
  speed — its own stated design goal (the whole point of "tweet"-sized crypto)
  uses a naive schoolbook field-multiplication routine rather than the optimized
  arithmetic (e.g., radix-51) that standard X25519 reference implementations
  like ref10 or curve25519-donna use. This isn't a property of X25519 the
  algorithm — it's specific to this implementation choice.

  Two ways to proceed:

  1. Keep TweetNaCl, but document loudly that this reflects TweetNaCl's known
  size-over-speed tradeoff, not a general X25519-vs-ML-KEM finding — still a
  legitimate, honest data point (and its ~21 KB flash footprint, half of
  ML-KEM's, is a genuinely fair and interesting size comparison).
  2. Swap to a faster reference implementation (e.g., curve25519-donna) for a
  timing comparison more representative of typical X25519 performance, at the
  cost of more integration work.

  My recommendation: keep TweetNaCl's code-size number (that comparison is fair
  as-is), but swap the timing benchmark to a standard-speed reference
  implementation so the performance comparison isn't misleading. Want me to
  proceed with that, or keep TweetNaCl for everything with the caveat clearly
  documented?
